Your Team Can't Find Information — Here's Why (and the Fix)

NOV 25, 2026 · Sola AI Consulting

The Real Cost of "I Didn't Know Where to Look"

It happens quietly. Someone asks a coworker for a file they already have. A new hire spends two hours hunting down a policy document that lives in three different places — none of them official. A scheduler makes a decision based on outdated information because the updated version never got communicated clearly.

When your team doesn't know where to find information, you don't just lose time. You lose trust in your systems. People start working from memory, from group texts, from whatever happened to land in their inbox last. The organization keeps moving, but it's running on guesswork.

This isn't a laziness problem. It's not even a training problem, most of the time. It's a structure problem — and it's completely fixable.

"When we first audited one home health agency's internal docs, we found the same intake form in seven different locations — four of them outdated. Staff weren't disorganized. The system was."

Why Your Team Doesn't Know Where to Find Information

If you've ever said "we sent that out already" or "it's in the shared drive," you've probably also noticed that it didn't actually solve the problem. That's because information access isn't just about storage — it's about findability, trust, and habit.

Too Many Places, No Single Source of Truth

Most small businesses have information scattered across email threads, Google Drive folders, shared network drives, sticky notes, and someone's desktop. Each tool made sense when it was added. Together, they create chaos. When everything is a possible source, nothing is a reliable one.

Your team stops looking because looking doesn't reliably work. That's rational behavior in a broken system.

The Shared Drive Illusion

A lot of operators assume that having a shared drive means having a knowledge base. It doesn't. A shared drive is a filing cabinet. A knowledge base is a system designed around how people actually search for things — by role, by task, by question.

When someone is onboarding a new caregiver, they don't search for "HR > Onboarding > 2023 Revisions > Final_v3." They type something like "what do I give a new hire on day one?" If your system can't answer that, the shared drive isn't solving your problem.

No Ownership, No Maintenance

Even well-organized systems decay fast when nobody owns them. Documents go stale. Folders multiply. The one person who knew where everything lived leaves the company, and suddenly the whole operation feels unstable.

Good information systems assign clear ownership — a person or a small team responsible for keeping things current and accurate. Without that, entropy wins every time.

Key takeaway

Pick one authoritative location for each type of information — policies, procedures, templates, contacts — and make sure every team member knows exactly where that one place is. Consistency beats comprehensiveness.

What a Real Fix Looks Like

When we work with clients at Sola AI Consulting, the first step isn't technology. It's mapping. We ask: what does your team actually need to look up, and when do they need it?

For a home health agency, that might be:

  • Caregiver onboarding checklists and HR paperwork
  • Scheduling policies and shift swap procedures
  • Client care protocols and emergency contacts
  • Billing codes and documentation requirements
  • State compliance documents and training records

Once you know what people are searching for, you can design a system around those actual needs — not around how your files happen to be organized right now.

Build a Staff Portal, Not Just a Folder

A staff portal is different from a file repository. It's a destination with a front door. It answers questions. It surfaces the right information based on role. It tells a new coordinator something different than it tells a field caregiver, because they need different things.

We build these in SharePoint for most of our clients because it integrates with Microsoft 365, works on mobile, and doesn't require your team to learn a new platform. But the tool matters less than the design. A bad SharePoint setup is just a confusing shared drive with better branding.

AI Search Changes the Game

One of the most practical upgrades we've added for clients is an AI-powered search layer on top of their portal. Instead of clicking through folders, staff type a question in plain English and get a direct answer — pulled from your actual documents, not from the internet.

"What's the overtime policy?" returns the paragraph from your employee handbook. "Who do I call if a client refuses care?" pulls up your incident response protocol. This isn't futuristic. It's available now, and it cuts "I didn't know where to find it" complaints dramatically.

Starting Small Without Starting Over

You don't have to rebuild everything at once. In fact, trying to do that usually means nothing gets done.

Here's a practical sequence that works:

  1. Audit what you have. Identify the five to ten documents your team references most often. Where do they currently live? Are they current?
  2. Pick one home base. Choose the platform your team already uses — Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, whatever it is — and commit to making that the official source of truth.
  3. Structure by task, not by department. Organize information around what people are trying to do, not around your org chart.
  4. Announce it clearly. Tell your team this is now the place. Reinforce it. When someone asks where something is, tell them — and also add it to the portal if it isn't there yet.
  5. Assign an owner. Even if it's just one person spending two hours a month maintaining it, that's enough to prevent decay.

The goal isn't perfection. It's predictability. When your team knows exactly where to look — and trusts that what they find is accurate — the whole operation runs smoother. Decisions happen faster. Onboarding takes less time. You stop answering the same questions over and over.

If your team doesn't know where to find information today, that's a signal that your internal infrastructure hasn't caught up with how your business actually operates. The good news is that fixing it doesn't require a large budget or a long implementation — just a clear plan and a commitment to maintaining it.
