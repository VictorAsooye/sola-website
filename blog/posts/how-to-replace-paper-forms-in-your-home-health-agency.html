How to Replace Paper Forms in Your Home Health Agency

JUL 17, 2026 · Sola AI Consulting

The Paper Problem Most Home Health Agencies Won't Admit

Walk into the back office of almost any small home health agency and you'll find the same thing: stacks of printed intake forms, visit notes scribbled in margins, fax confirmations stapled to authorizations, and somewhere in that pile, a document nobody can find when the auditor calls.

Paper forms feel safe because they're familiar. But they're costing you more than you think — in staff time, compliance risk, and the kind of small errors that compound quietly until they become very expensive problems.

When we work with home health agencies at Sola AI Consulting, one of the first things we do is map out every paper form the agency touches in a week. Most owners are surprised. The number is almost always higher than they expected — intake packets, OASIS addendums, physician order fax logs, employee incident reports, mileage sheets. It adds up fast.

"We were spending about four hours every Monday just re-entering information that caregivers had already written down on paper over the weekend. We didn't even realize that was optional."

— Director of Operations, Maryland home health agency

The good news: replacing paper forms in a home health agency is not a massive IT project. It's a series of small, deliberate swaps — and most agencies can start seeing results within a few weeks.

Where to Start When You Replace Paper Forms

The mistake most agencies make is trying to digitize everything at once. That leads to decision fatigue, staff resistance, and half-finished implementations that end up making things worse than before.

Start with your highest-volume, highest-pain forms. Ask yourself two questions: Which forms are filled out most often? And which ones cause the most problems when they're lost, incomplete, or illegible?

Intake and Referral Packets

This is usually the right place to begin. Every new client requires the same core information — demographics, insurance, emergency contacts, physician details, care authorization. When that lives on paper, someone has to physically handle it, file it, and then re-enter parts of it into your billing or scheduling software.

A digital intake form — built in Microsoft Forms, a SharePoint-connected form, or a dedicated tool — eliminates the re-entry step entirely. The information goes in once. It routes automatically. It sits in a searchable record instead of a manila folder.

Visit Verification and Daily Notes

Paper visit logs create a lag. A caregiver finishes a shift, fills out a note on paper, and that note doesn't reach your office until their next visit — or whenever they remember to drop it off. By then, you've already made scheduling decisions without it.

Digital visit notes, whether through a simple mobile form or EVV-integrated software, give you real-time visibility. You know what happened today, not what happened four days ago.

HR and Compliance Documents

Employee files in home health are dense — TB tests, CPR certifications, background check acknowledgments, I-9s, competency checklists. Most agencies track expiration dates in a spreadsheet they remember to check inconsistently.

When those documents live in a structured digital system, you can set automated reminders. Nothing expires without warning. Audits become a matter of pulling a report instead of hunting through a filing cabinet.

Key takeaway

Don't try to replace every paper form at once. Pick the three forms your office touches most often and digitize those first. Get staff comfortable with the new process before expanding. Slow rollouts stick better than fast ones.

What Replacing Paper Forms Actually Looks Like in Practice

Here's a realistic picture of what a phased transition looks like for a small home health agency — say, 15 to 40 clients, 10 to 20 caregivers, and a two- or three-person office team.

Month One: Audit and Map

Before you build anything, spend two to three weeks documenting every form you use, who fills it out, where it goes, and what happens to it after. This sounds tedious. It is, briefly. But it prevents you from digitizing a broken process and calling it an improvement.

We use a simple spreadsheet for this — form name, frequency, who owns it, downstream destination, and current pain points. By the end of the audit, the priorities become obvious.

Month Two: Build and Test

For most small agencies, Microsoft 365 is already in the environment. That means Microsoft Forms and Power Automate are available at no additional cost. A well-built intake form connected to a SharePoint list can replace a paper packet and trigger automatic notifications to the right people — all within tools you already pay for.

Build one form. Test it with one or two staff members. Fix what doesn't work before rolling it out to the team.

Month Three: Train and Retire the Paper

Training is where most digital transitions fail. Staff get a five-minute walkthrough and then are left to figure it out on their own. They fall back on paper because it's faster in the moment, even if it costs more in the long run.

Good training for a small agency doesn't need to be elaborate. A short screen recording showing exactly how to fill out the form. A written reference they can keep at their desk. A clear answer to "what do I do if this doesn't work?" That's usually enough.

Once staff are comfortable, retire the paper version. Not "we'll phase it out gradually." Retire it. The parallel period is where confusion lives.

Compliance, Security, and the Questions Owners Actually Ask

When home health agency owners start thinking seriously about going digital, compliance questions come up fast — and they should. This is a regulated industry. You handle protected health information. The stakes for a documentation error are real.

Is digital documentation compliant with state and federal requirements?

Yes — when it's done correctly. Digital forms must be part of a secure, access-controlled system. Data needs to be backed up. Access logs matter. Sensitive fields should require authentication.

Microsoft 365 environments, configured properly, meet HIPAA standards for document storage and workflow. Many agencies are already using these tools but haven't locked them down appropriately. That's a fixable problem, and it's worth addressing before you expand your digital footprint.

What happens during an audit?

This is actually where going digital helps you most. When a surveyor asks for the last six months of visit notes for a specific client, the answer should be a filtered search and a printout — not an afternoon of digging through boxes.

Structured digital records are easier to retrieve, easier to organize, and easier to present than paper. Agencies that have made the switch consistently report that audits feel less stressful — not because the documentation is different, but because they can actually find it.

If you're ready to stop managing paper and start running a tighter, more transparent operation, the path forward is clearer than it might seem. The technology is accessible. The process is learnable. And the relief that comes from knowing your documentation is organized, searchable, and secure is the kind of thing owners don't expect until they feel it for the first time.
